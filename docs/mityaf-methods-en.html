How the Method Works,
Piece by Piece

Every term in the technical pitch, explained from first principles — what a DEM is, what HAND actually measures, why we trained on American cities, and why the simpler method is the one that ships.

Companion to the methodology section · all figures traceable to research/

This document exists because the pitch compresses about six distinct ideas into ninety seconds. Each of them is simple on its own. What follows takes them one at a time, in the order the system uses them, with nothing assumed.

The one-sentence version

We read the shape of the ground from a free satellite elevation model, turn that shape into nine numbers for every street segment in Al Madinah, and use those numbers to rank all 30,337 segments by how likely they are to hold standing water after rain. No sensors, no ground labels from the city itself.

01The foundation: what a DEM is

A Digital Elevation Model is a grid laid over the earth where every cell holds one number: the height of the ground at that spot, in metres above sea level. Think of a spreadsheet the size of a city, where cell (412, 388) contains 619.4.

Ours is Copernicus DEM GLO-30, published free by the European Space Agency. Over Al Madinah it is a grid of 817 × 818 cells, each cell 30 metres across. That resolution is the single most important constraint in the whole system, so it is worth being concrete: one cell is roughly the width of a six-lane road plus its verges. Anything narrower than 30 m — a kerb, a gutter, a single blocked drain — does not exist in this data.

A distinction that matters later

GLO-30 is a surface model (DSM), not a bare-earth terrain model (DTM). It records the top of whatever the radar hit — including bridges and overpasses. This is exactly why underpasses are invisible to it, and why they need a separate rule. Hold that thought for §7.

From heights to features

A raw height is nearly useless on its own. Knowing a street sits at 619 m tells you nothing — the whole city sits around 600 m. What predicts ponding is the relationship between a point and the ground around it. So we derive six quantities from the height grid. Each one answers a different question about that relationship.

02The six terrain features, one at a time

sink Depression depth metres

The question it answers
If you poured water here, how deep would it pool before spilling out?
How it is computed
Take the height grid and algorithmically fill every closed hollow until water could drain off the edge — a standard operation called priority-flood depression filling. Then subtract the original grid from the filled one. Where the two differ, that difference is the depth of the bowl at that point. Where the land already drained freely, the difference is zero.
Why it predicts ponding
It is the most physically direct predictor there is. A closed hollow with no outlet is, definitionally, where water stays. This is the literal puddle depth the land would hold.
What to watch
Most of the city reads 0.00, because most ground drains somewhere. A non-zero sink is already unusual.

hand Height Above Nearest Drainage metres

The question it answers
How high does this spot sit above the nearest place water naturally flows to?
How it is computed
First trace the drainage network — the lines water would follow downhill across the whole grid. Then, for every cell, follow its own downhill path until it meets that network, and record the height difference between the cell and the point where it joins. That difference is HAND.
Why it predicts ponding
It is drainage potential expressed as a single number. A street 12 m above the nearest channel sheds water easily — gravity has somewhere to take it. A street at −2 m, below the local drainage line, has nowhere to send it.
The sign is the whole point
Negative HAND means the ground sits below its own drainage line. That is the condition the first mechanism rule tests for.

slope Steepness degrees or %

The question it answers
How steeply does the ground tilt here?
How it is computed
Compare each cell's height with its neighbours and take the steepest rate of change.
Why it predicts ponding
Water leaves a slope and lingers on the flat. Slope is the brake: a 1° street sheds water slowly, a 6° street sheds it immediately. Low slope is a necessary condition for standing water — but nowhere near sufficient on its own, which is why it never acts alone.

logacc Flow accumulation log of cell count

The question it answers
How much upstream land drains through this spot?
How it is computed
Send one unit of water into every cell of the grid, let it all run downhill, and count how many units pass through each cell. A ridge-top cell accumulates 1. A wadi bed accumulates hundreds of thousands. Because the range spans orders of magnitude, we take the logarithm.
Why it predicts ponding
It measures how much water arrives. Depth of hollow tells you the container; flow accumulation tells you the supply filling it.

twi Topographic Wetness Index dimensionless

The question it answers
Combining supply and drainage, how wet does this spot tend to be?
How it is computed
TWI = ln( a / tan β ), where a is the upslope area draining through the point and β is the local slope. It comes from Beven & Kirkby's 1979 hydrology work and is one of the most established indices in the field.
Why it predicts ponding
Read the formula as a ratio: a lot of water arriving divided by how fast the ground lets it leave. Large catchment over gentle slope gives a high number and a wet place. It captures in one value what logacc and slope say separately.

relelev Relative elevation metres

The question it answers
Is this spot lower or higher than its immediate surroundings?
How it is computed
Subtract the average height of a neighbourhood window from the cell's own height. Negative means a local dip.
Why it predicts ponding
It complements sink. Depression depth requires a fully closed bowl; relative elevation catches the shallow, open-ended dip that still collects water but would fill and spill.

03The three urban-form features

Terrain decides where water can collect. It says nothing about whether anyone is there to care. Three more features come from OpenStreetMap — the free, volunteer-built world map — and describe the built environment instead.

road_density Road density

What it is
How much road length packs into the area around this segment.
Why it matters
A proxy for hard, sealed surface. Asphalt does not absorb; every drop that lands on it has to go somewhere as runoff.

major_frac Major-road share

What it is
What proportion of nearby roads are arterials rather than residential streets.
Why it matters
Arterials are wider, more sealed, and carry junctions and grade separations — the built forms most likely to sit below their surroundings.

load_per_road Load per road

What it is
How much drainage burden falls on each unit of road in the area.
Why it matters
Distinguishes a dense grid sharing the load from a single road carrying an entire catchment alone.

Six terrain features plus three urban features gives nine numbers per street segment — the complete description every method downstream works from. Al Madinah's road network divides into 30,337 segments, so the whole city is a table of 30,337 rows and nine columns.

04Path one: machine learning, and its obstacle

The obvious approach is supervised learning: show a model many streets that flooded and many that did not, and let it learn the pattern. This runs immediately into a wall.

The label problem

Al Madinah has no open register of flood reports with coordinates. Machine learning needs labelled examples — "this street flooded, that one did not". Without labels there is nothing to learn from. This is not a gap in our effort; it is the reason the problem is unsolved.

Borrowing labels from cities that have them

Four American cities publish exactly what Al Madinah lacks: citizen service requests, geocoded, categorised, spanning years. We used New York, Chicago, San Francisco and Dallas — together 130,998 reports of the same physical phenomenon: water standing on a street after rain.

The bet is that the physics is portable even though the city is not. A closed hollow below its drainage line holds water in Brooklyn for the same reason it does in حي الملك فهد. The nine features are deliberately chosen to describe only that physics — nothing about zoning, building codes or climate that would not carry across.

How we tested it honestly

Testing on data you trained on tells you nothing. We used leave-one-city-out: train on three cities, then predict the fourth — a city the model has never seen, with different terrain, different street layout, different rainfall.

Reading the scores

Recall — of the streets that really flooded, what proportion did the model flag? Ours averages 0.83 across the held-out cities. This is the number the pitch quotes.

F1 — the balance between catching real floods and not crying wolf. 0.84 in San Francisco; lower elsewhere, and we name the city rather than quoting the best figure unqualified.

Accuracy is deliberately not quoted. Flooded cells are about 0.036% of the data. A model that says "nothing ever floods" scores 99.96% accuracy and is worthless. Any accuracy figure here would be meaningless, so we do not use one.

05Path two: a hydrology ranking with no training at all

Alongside the models we built something deliberately primitive: a ranking computed straight from the physics, with no learned parameters and no labels.

# every term is a percentile rank across the city, 0–1 rank_hydrology = pct( pct(twi) + pct(sink) − pct(hand) − pct(slope) )

Read it in plain language: wetter is worse, deeper hollow is worse, higher above drainage is better, steeper is better. Four terms, equal weight, nothing fitted to anything. You can check it by hand.

06The real test: which one survives in Al Madinah?

Both paths now had to face the city itself. We assembled ground truth manually — official statements, news reports and resident accounts — reaching eleven distinct flood locations with dates and coordinates. Four of those coordinates are precise enough to trust completely.

What AUC means with eleven points

With only positive examples and no verified dry streets, we use AUC in its rank interpretation: the average percentile position of the known flood sites within the city-wide ranking. 0.5 is a coin flip — the sites land wherever chance puts them. 0.685 means they consistently sit in the worse-ranked part of the city. Nothing here is a probability of flooding.

Why the simpler method ships

Not because machine learning failed — it transfers respectably. Because the simpler thing was truer, and it carries three practical advantages the models cannot match: it needs zero labels from Al Madinah, a municipal engineer can audit all four terms by eye, and there is no training distribution to drift away from.

The trained models remain in the platform as a second opinion. They are not what produces the ranking.

07The output, and the three mechanisms

Ranking alone says which street is worst. It does not say why — and without why, there is no action. So the shortlist is classified by three explicit rules. These are conditions, not a trained model.

The third rule is the odd one out, and §1 explained why: the DEM measures the deck over the road, not the road. At these 47 segments the elevation model reads ordinary flat ground. Rule 1 would have caught only 9 of them, and the model puts 22 above the drainage line. 38 of 47 are invisible to the terrain data — which is precisely why this class must be read from a map tag instead.

From city to work order

Of 30,337 segments, 2,617 — just 8.6% — carry a mechanism and warrant inspection. The remaining 27,720 still hold a rank; they simply match no rule. That reduction is the product: a crew can inspect 8.6% of a city before a season. It cannot inspect all of it.

08What this system cannot do

Stating the limits is part of the method, not a caveat appended to it.

  • It is not validated in Al Madinah. Eleven ground-truth points is evidence, not proof. The honest claim is a consistent signal at p = 0.016, not a measured accuracy.
  • It predicts susceptibility, not events. The output is "this street holds water when it rains", never "this street will flood on Tuesday".
  • 30 m is the floor. A blocked drain, a bad kerb, a construction diversion — none are visible at this resolution.
  • The elevation data is from 2011–2015. Terrain changes slowly, but a district built since then is described by the ground as it was before it existed.
  • Percentile, never probability. A segment ranked 0.98 is in the worst 2% of this city. It is not "98% likely to flood".
  • Street names are largely absent. OpenStreetMap names only 1.1% of Al Madinah's residential ways, so most segments are identified by their nearest named road and a coordinate rather than a name.
The claim, stated exactly

We rank every street in Al Madinah by how likely it is to hold standing water, using free public data and no sensors; we classify 2,617 of them into three mechanisms with three different remedies; and we do it with a method simple enough that a municipal engineer can check it by hand.
