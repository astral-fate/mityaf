Chapter · Al Madinah Urban Water Intelligence

Measuring Where Water Will Stand

Satellite-derived street-flood susceptibility for a city that has never recorded a flood

Mityaf (مِطياف) · Barmajan Al Munawwarah 2026

Abstract

Al Madinah receives a millimetre or more of rain on roughly 2.3% of days — about eight days a year — and those eight days are what close its streets. Because rain is rare, the city never accumulates an operational memory of where water stands, so each storm is handled as though it were the first. Response is reactive: a street floods, somebody reports it, a tanker arrives. A neighbourhood that does not complain never appears on any map.

This chapter describes a system that ranks all 30,337 street segments in the city by their susceptibility to standing water, using only a digital elevation model and an open street map — no ground sensors, no local flood records, and no satellite imagery of flooding itself. The central methodological problem is that Al Madinah has no labelled flood incidents at all. We therefore do not train a model on Al Madinah. Instead we ask a narrower, testable question: do the nine features we can compute for Al Madinah predict street flooding in cities that do have records? Using 101,756 geolocated citizen reports from New York and Chicago, under spatially blocked cross-validation and a leave-one-city-out transfer test, the answer is yes — 1.44× and 1.45× over baseline within cities, and 1.24× and 1.30× when transferred to a city the model has never seen.

The chapter explains, from first principles and in plain language, where the elevation data comes from and how a radar satellite measures ground height; what each of the nine features means physically; why accuracy is the wrong metric and what PR-AUC measures instead; why the choice of negative samples mattered more than the choice of algorithm; and what the resulting rankings can and cannot tell a municipal engineer.

Section 1Introduction

The problem is not that water floods streets. The problem is that nobody knows which streets before it happens.

1.1The situation

When rain falls on a city, not all of it reaches a drain. Some of it stands. And it stands in particular places that repeat every season: a closed depression with no outlet, a street lower than its surroundings, a point where the runoff of many streets above converges.

In Al Madinah, this happens on about eight days a year. The city sits in an arid zone where rain of a millimetre or more — the World Meteorological Organization’s threshold for a rain day — falls on roughly 2.3% of days. Days heavy enough to actually stand on a road, above 5 mm, are fewer still: two or three a year. That rarity is usually described as a mitigating factor. It is the opposite. Because storms are rare, the municipality never builds an institutional memory of where water collects. There is no dataset of past ponding locations, no accumulated map, no list. Each rain event is therefore managed from scratch, and the knowledge of where the water went evaporates with the water.

How many rainy days, exactly?

A “rainy day” is not a self-defining quantity — it depends entirely on the threshold you count at. The World Meteorological Organization counts a day as a rain day when it receives 1 mm or more, and that is the threshold used throughout this chapter. Because the distinction changes the headline figure by a factor of three, we verified it against five independent sources for the city centroid (24.47°N, 39.61°E):

The airport gauge reports more, but only a third of its days since 2015 carry a precipitation value at all and its annual total is roughly double the reanalyses’, so its wet days are almost certainly over-represented; we report it for completeness and do not rely on it.

The threshold matters more than the choice of source. Counting any measurable trace roughly triples the count, to somewhere between thirteen and twenty-five days — but a few tenths of a millimetre does not stand on a street. Days above 5 mm, which do, number two to three a year. Read that way the scarcity argument below is sharper, not softer.

What fills the vacuum is the complaint. A street floods; a resident is affected; the resident reports it through Balady+ or the 940 line; a tanker is dispatched to pump the water away. The response is competent and it is entirely reactive. It begins after the damage, and it acts on the subset of locations that somebody chose to report.

The gap this chapter addresses

A municipality that owns 5,280 km of street network and a finite number of crews needs to know where to look first — before the season, not after the storm. That is a ranking problem, not a forecasting problem, and it turns out to be solvable with data that is free and globally available.

1.2Why this is methodologically hard

The obvious approach would be to collect Al Madinah's historical flood records and train a model on them. This is impossible: those records do not exist. Nobody surveys the city during a storm and writes down where the water stood. There is no labelled dataset, which means there is nothing to train on and — more importantly — nothing to test against.

This constraint is usually hidden. A model can always be fitted to something, and a number can always be reported. But a model trained without labels and then declared "accurate" has been measured against nothing at all. Rather than conceal the problem, we let it define the research question. Instead of asking how do we build a model for Al Madinah?, we asked:

Do the features we can compute for Al Madinah — from terrain and urban form alone, with no field data whatsoever — actually predict street flooding in cities that do have field data?

If the answer is no, the product is built on sand and should not be built. If the answer is yes, then what transfers to Al Madinah is the shape of the model — the feature definitions, the sampling rule, the validation protocol, the physical relationships — and explicitly not the fitted weights. Sections 8 through 10 describe how we tested that, and what it cost.

1.3What this chapter covers

Sections 2 and 3 place the work in context: what pluvial flooding is, how it differs from the flash floods that dominate Saudi headlines, and what the research literature already establishes about predicting it. Section 4 documents every data source in detail — including how a radar satellite actually measures the height of the ground, what file format the measurement arrives in, and the significant limitation that the elevation model includes buildings. Section 5 presents the exploratory analysis. Sections 6 and 7 define the nine features and the model. Sections 8 and 9 cover validation and metrics, both explained from first principles. Section 10 reports results, Section 11 applies them to Al Madinah, and Sections 12–14 describe the delivered system, its limits, and what comes next.

1.4What is not claimed

Precision about claims matters more in this domain than in most, because the failure mode is not an inaccurate map — it is a municipality reallocating budget on the basis of one. Four claims are explicitly not made anywhere in this work:

  • Not a forecast. The model does not say a street will flood on a given date. It ranks susceptibility, which is a property of place, not of weather.
  • Not a depth estimate. Nothing here distinguishes five centimetres of water from half a metre.
  • Not a flash-flood or wadi model. Section 2.1 draws this boundary carefully, because the most publicised Saudi flood events are of exactly the kind this system does not address.
  • Not superior to published work. Section 10.5 explains why no such comparison is meaningful under the current state of the literature.

Section 2Background: Urban Pluvial Flooding

Three different phenomena are all called "flooding" in ordinary speech. Distinguishing them determines what can be modelled and what cannot.

2.1Pluvial, fluvial, and flash flooding

Fluvial flooding occurs when a river or channel exceeds its banks. It is driven by the water balance of an entire upstream catchment, and it is the classic subject of hydraulic modelling.

Flash flooding is a rapid, high-energy wave travelling down a normally dry channel — in Arabia, a wadi. It carries debris, moves vehicles, and destroys infrastructure. It is the phenomenon behind almost every fatal Saudi flood event in the public record.

Pluvial flooding — sometimes called surface-water or nuisance flooding — is rain that falls on a surface and cannot drain away from where it lands. No river is involved. The water is local, usually shallow, and its location is governed by very local topography and by how much of the surface is impervious. This is the subject of this chapter, and it is the phenomenon that most affects daily life in a city like Al Madinah.

Saudi Arabia's documented flood disasters are overwhelmingly of the second kind. They are worth stating precisely, both because they explain why flood risk is a national priority and because they mark the boundary of what this work addresses.

Boundary statement

The Jazan bridge collapse and the Jeddah disasters are wadi flash floods. This system would not have predicted or prevented any of them. It does not model channel hydraulics, wave propagation, bridge scour, or structural safety. Citing these events as motivation while implying the model addresses them would be a misrepresentation, and this chapter does not make it.

The connection that is real: the same terrain descriptors used here — height above nearest drainage, closed-depression depth, flow accumulation — also determine which streets lie in the path of, or in depressions fed by, wadi overflow. The tool is relevant to the same hazard family at a different scale, with a different and narrower claim.

2.3Al Madinah's hydrology

Seven wadis cross Al Madinah. Wadi Al-Aqiq is the most storied. Wadi Bathan runs through the centre toward the north-west and is formed from four smaller valleys. Wadi Mudhainib is a branch of Bathan, sharing its source at Dhu al-Jadr. Wadi Qanat is the largest, originating from Wadi Waj near Taif. Al-Aqiq, Qanat and Bathan converge at Al-Zaghabah north-west of the city to form Wadi Al-Hamd.

This matters for two reasons. First, a city built across seven drainage lines has a terrain surface whose natural flow paths run through, not around, the urban fabric. Second — and this is a detail worth noticing — Wadi Mudhainib is also the name of a residential district, and it appears in this system's output among the ten most exposed districts, with 128 segments in the high or critical bands. A neighbourhood named after a valley, surfacing in a risk ranking computed from terrain alone.

2.4Why rarity makes the problem worse

Intuitively, a city that rarely floods has a smaller problem. Operationally the reverse holds, for three reasons.

  1. No institutional memory forms. Learning requires repetition close enough in time to connect cause and effect. Eight events a year, spread across a 545 km² city, means any given street segment may flood once in several years — too sparse for anyone to accumulate a reliable mental map.
  2. No dataset accumulates. Rare events produce few records, and few records mean no basis for statistical learning. This is precisely the constraint that shaped this study.
  3. Infrastructure is sized for the average, tested by the maximum. The heaviest day in Al Madinah's rainfall archive is 65.6 mm. A drainage network adequate on a typical rainy day may be irrelevant on that one.

Climate projections make this sharper. Studies of Saudi rainfall report a consistent pattern of intensifying extremes alongside declining annual totals [15][16] — fewer rain days, heavier peaks. That is the exact direction that degrades a system tuned to averages, and it is the pattern Al Madinah already exhibits.

Meanwhile the Kingdom is investing heavily in the physical response. The Ministry of Municipalities and Housing laid more than 454,000 linear metres of stormwater drainage in 2024 alone, against 291,000 m in 2023, for a programme total exceeding eight million linear metres. Yet no independent instrument measures whether any given scheme worked after commissioning; success is assessed from the contractor's own report.

Section 3Literature Review

Four bodies of work meet in this problem: terrain hydrology, machine-learning susceptibility mapping, citizen reports as data, and the statistics of rare events. Each contributes a method; two contribute a warning.

3.1Terrain indices: the TOPMODEL lineage

The idea that the shape of the land predicts where water accumulates is not new. Beven and Kirkby's TOPMODEL [1] introduced the Topographic Wetness Index (TWI), which combines how much upslope area drains through a point with how steeply that point sits:

TWI = ln( a / tan β )   where a = upslope contributing area per unit contour width, β = local slope

The logic is direct. A large upslope area delivers a lot of water; a shallow slope removes it slowly. Divide one by the other and high values mark places that receive much and shed little. TWI has been used for decades in catchment hydrology and soil moisture mapping.

Computing a requires knowing which way water flows across every cell of an elevation grid. The standard method is the D8 algorithm of O'Callaghan and Mark [2]: each cell drains to whichever of its eight neighbours lies steepest downhill. Accumulating these single-direction flows downstream gives, for every cell, the number of upstream cells that drain through it.

D8 has a well-known failure. Real elevation grids contain pits — cells lower than all their neighbours, some genuine depressions and some measurement noise. Flow routing terminates in them, breaking the accumulation. The conventional remedy is depression filling, and the efficient modern algorithm is priority-flood (Barnes, Lehman and Mulla [3]), which floods the surface inward from the boundary using a priority queue, raising each unvisited cell to its outlet level in a single pass. Crucially for this work, the amount by which each cell is raised is itself meaningful: it is the depth of the closed depression at that point.

A second descriptor, HAND (Height Above Nearest Drainage), was introduced by Rennó et al. [4] and developed by Nobre et al. [5]. Rather than absolute elevation, HAND measures how high a point sits above the nearest channel that drains it. It normalises terrain to the local water level and has become a standard input to flood-hazard mapping because it correlates strongly with inundation extent while requiring only a DEM.

3.2Terrain indices in urban settings

Transferring these indices from catchments to cities is not automatic, and the literature is appropriately cautious. Urban surfaces are engineered: the flow path a DEM implies may be interrupted by a kerb, a culvert, or a storm drain that the elevation model cannot see. Kelleher and McPhillips [6] examined exactly this question — whether topographic indices identify pluvial flooding locations in urban landscapes — and found the relationship real but weaker and noisier than in natural catchments, precisely because subsurface drainage is invisible to terrain analysis.

This is a finding this work inherits rather than disputes. It sets a ceiling on achievable performance and explains why the results reported in Section 10 are modest multiples of baseline rather than the near-perfect scores that appear in some published susceptibility studies.

3.3Machine learning for flood susceptibility

A large literature applies statistical and machine-learning classifiers to flood susceptibility mapping — weights-of-evidence, support vector machines, random forests, and gradient boosting, typically over a stack of terrain and land-cover rasters [7]. Tree ensembles dominate recent work because the relationships are strongly non-linear and interacting: depression depth matters enormously at small values and then saturates, and its effect depends on whether the surface is paved.

Gradient boosting as implemented in XGBoost [8] is the specific method used here; Section 7 explains what it does. Its practical advantages for this problem are that it handles heterogeneous numeric features without scaling, tolerates moderate class imbalance through instance weighting, and — importantly for a municipal deployment — exposes a per-feature gain attribution that can be shown to an engineer as a reason.

3.4Citizen reports as ground truth

The absence of authoritative flood inventories in most cities has pushed researchers toward crowd-sourced and administrative complaint data. New York City's 311 service request archive has become something of a reference dataset: several studies use its street-flooding descriptor to study spatial patterns of urban pluvial flooding [9][10], including machine-learning analyses of its spatial variability [10].

These datasets have a well-understood weakness, which Section 13 returns to: a complaint records where somebody reported water, which is not the same as where water was. Reporting propensity varies with tenure, language, age, and trust in municipal services. Any model trained on complaints partially learns who complains.

3.5Trap one: spatial autocorrelation

Terrain variables are strongly spatially autocorrelated — adjacent cells share slope, catchment, and imperviousness almost by definition. If training and test sets are formed by random splitting, neighbouring and nearly identical cells land on both sides. The model can then achieve a high score by effectively memorising locations it has already seen, and the reported metric measures interpolation rather than prediction.

This is not a subtle effect. Roberts et al. [11] set out blocking strategies for exactly this situation, and Ploton et al. [12] demonstrated in Nature Communications that spatially naive validation can make ecological models look excellent while their true out-of-sample predictive power is close to nil. The corrective is spatially blocked cross-validation: partition space into contiguous blocks and hold out whole blocks, so that no test cell has a training neighbour.

Why this matters for reading any susceptibility paper

A published ROC-AUC of 0.95 under random k-fold and a PR-AUC of 0.40 under spatial blocking can describe the same model on the same data. When comparing results across studies, the validation protocol matters more than the headline number.

3.6Trap two: presence-only data

Complaint archives are presence-only: they record where flooding was reported and say nothing about where it was absent. Supervised classification needs negatives, so negatives must be constructed — and how they are constructed determines what question the model ends up answering.

This problem is best developed in species distribution modelling, where the same structure arises. Phillips et al. [17] showed that sample selection bias in presence-only data systematically distorts fitted models, and Barbet-Massin et al. [18] gave practical guidance on choosing pseudo-absences: draw them from the same environmental domain in which presences could have been observed, or the classifier learns the sampling process instead of the phenomenon. Section 8.1 shows what this meant concretely here — and it turned out to matter more than the choice of algorithm.

3.7Evaluation under class imbalance

When positives are a minority, accuracy becomes meaningless and ROC-AUC becomes misleadingly flattering, because the large number of true negatives dominates the false positive rate. Davis and Goadrich [19] established the formal relationship between ROC and precision–recall space, and Saito and Rehmsmeier [20] demonstrated that the precision–recall plot is the more informative of the two on imbalanced data. Section 9 develops this from first principles, because interpreting the results in Section 10 depends entirely on it.

3.8Where this work sits

The contribution here is not a new algorithm or a new terrain index. It is a transfer result under a deliberately harsh protocol, addressing a situation the literature rarely confronts: a target city with zero labels. The question — can a feature set computable for an unlabelled city be validated on labelled cities, and how much predictive power survives the move — is, as far as we can determine, not answered elsewhere for urban pluvial flooding.

Section 4The Data

Every input is free, open, and globally available. That is a design constraint, not a convenience: a feature that cannot be reproduced for Al Madinah is useless no matter how well it performs in New York.

How a radar satellite measures ground height

This is not a photograph. No camera is involved, and the measurement does not depend on sunlight. Two spacecraft fly a few hundred metres apart. One transmits a microwave pulse toward the ground; both receive the echo. Because the two receivers sit at slightly different positions, the returning wave arrives at each with a slightly different phase — the point in its oscillation cycle. That phase difference is a function of the path-length difference, and path length depends on how far away the ground is. Measure the phase difference across the whole scene and you can solve for the height of every point on the surface.

Because microwaves pass through cloud and need no illumination, the technique works at night and in any weather — which is why global coverage was achievable at all.

Is it updated? No.

The data is a snapshot, not a stream. Acquisition ended in 2015. Nine releases were issued between 2019 and 2024, but these are corrective and refinement maintenance of the same measurements, not new capture.

Practically this is mostly fine: valleys, depressions and slopes do not change measurably in a decade. The real consequence is that construction after 2015 is absent from the model — a genuine limitation in a rapidly expanding city, and one recorded in Section 13.

4.2The most important limitation: surface, not ground

Copernicus DEM is a DSM — a Digital Surface Model. It records the top of whatever the radar hit, including buildings and vegetation. It is not a DTM, a Digital Terrain Model of the bare earth.

Water does not flow across rooftops. Computing flow accumulation over a DSM in a dense neighbourhood therefore routes some water over buildings rather than along the streets between them. This is the single largest source of physical error in the feature set.

Rather than note the concern and move on, we measured it. Comparing Copernicus against FABDEM — a bare-earth correction of the same product — across Al Madinah gave:

Higher-resolution commercial options exist: Airbus WorldDEM at 12 m and WorldDEM Neo at 5 m, both quoted commercially, and a national survey through GEOSA would be a government-to-government arrangement.

4.3The vector data: OpenStreetMap

Three features and the entire spatial unit definition come from OpenStreetMap, queried through the Overpass API. From it we take road centrelines with their highway classification (motorway, trunk, primary, secondary, tertiary, residential, unclassified), street names, and administrative district boundaries. The licence is ODbL, which requires attribution.

OSM is the only realistic option at this scope: it is the sole globally consistent, freely licensed street network with a classification scheme that means the same thing in New York, Chicago and Al Madinah. That consistency is what makes cross-city transfer testable at all.

4.4The labels: 311 complaint archives

Supervised learning needs examples of the outcome. Al Madinah has none, so the labels come from two American cities whose municipal complaint systems use a descriptor meaning, literally, standing water on a street surface.

The descriptor match is important. These are not river floods, not basement flooding, not building inundation — they are reports of water standing on a road. That is the same phenomenon the model is asked to rank in Al Madinah, which is what makes the labels appropriate rather than merely available.

Why two cities and not one

Spatial splitting within a single city answers does the model generalise across neighbourhoods? It cannot answer does it generalise across cities? — because within one city, drainage design, climate and reporting culture are all held constant. Those are precisely the things that differ in Al Madinah. A second city makes the transfer test possible.

Two further cities were examined and rejected, recorded here so the check is not repeated: San Francisco has no street-flooding category, only a building-level Light_Wells_Dirty_Flooded descriptor; Baltimore's Socrata resource did not respond.

4.5What the model actually sees

There are no images in this model

No satellite photograph, no aerial image, no picture of a flood. The model sees a table: one row per 200 m cell, nine numeric columns, and a 0/1 label. The rasters are inputs to feature computation, not inputs to the classifier.

This is worth stating because "satellite AI" invites the assumption of computer vision. There is none here. The satellite contributes a grid of elevation numbers; from that grid six statistics are computed per cell; three more come from vector geometry; and a gradient-boosted tree ensemble operates on the resulting nine columns.

Manhattan is the densest and flattest borough, and it produces the fewest street-flooding complaints. On a purely topographic account this should not happen. The literature attributes it to sewer design: Manhattan runs a combined sewer system carrying stormwater and wastewater in one pipe of substantial capacity, whereas the outer boroughs largely use separate storm sewers.

The methodological lesson, extracted early

Drainage design — invisible to any elevation model — governs outcomes as strongly as terrain does. This single observation is why the study reports transfer performance separately, and why the fitted weights are explicitly declared non-transferable while the feature definitions are.

5.2Choosing the spatial unit

Point complaints must be aggregated to an analysis unit. The choice is a genuine trade-off, and 200 m × 200 m cells were selected because that approximates the length of a single street segment.

  • Smaller cells cause a single complaint to migrate between adjacent cells depending on geocoding precision, adding label noise without adding information.
  • Larger cells merge streets with genuinely different behaviour — a flooding street and a dry one — into one row, diluting the signal.

Section 6Feature Engineering

Nine numbers per cell. Six describe the shape of the land; three describe what has been built on it.

6.1The hydrological six

slope — surface gradient, in degrees

The steepness of the ground, computed from the gradient of the elevation grid. Flat ground drains slowly; steep ground sheds water quickly. This is the simplest of the six and, as Section 10.3 shows, among the least important on its own.

sink — closed depression depth, in metres

The most physically direct predictor of ponding. A closed depression is a hollow with no outlet: water that enters cannot leave except by evaporation or pumping. Depth is obtained as a by-product of priority-flood depression filling [3] — the algorithm raises each pit cell to the level of its lowest outlet, and the amount of that rise is the depression depth at that point.

A documented bug worth recording

The first implementation used a naive iterative fill. Across 60 sweeps it raised cells by a maximum of 6 centimetres — meaning sink, the single most direct predictor of standing water, was effectively a dead feature contributing nothing. Replacing it with a correct priority-flood implementation produced a maximum depression depth of 43.92 m. A study that hides its errors should not be believed about its successes.

logacc — flow accumulation, log-transformed

How many upstream cells drain through this one, computed by D8 routing [2] in descending elevation order, then passed through log1p. The log transform is necessary because raw accumulation is extremely skewed — most cells drain a handful of neighbours while a few channel cells drain thousands.

twi — topographic wetness index

As defined in Section 3.1: ln(SCA / tan(slope)), where SCA is specific catchment area. It combines accumulation and slope into one number expressing a location's tendency to gather water.

hand — height above nearest drainage, in metres

How far this point sits above the nearest channel that would drain it. The drainage network is approximated as the top 0.5% of cells by flow accumulation. Low HAND means the point is close to local water level; high HAND means it sits well above it.

relelev — relative elevation, in metres

DEM − (17×17 moving mean). This asks a purely local question: is this point lower or higher than its immediate surroundings? A negative value marks a local hollow. It complements sink, which requires a fully closed depression, by capturing shallow local lows that are not sealed.

6.2The urban-form three

road_density — kilometres of road per km²

Each road centreline is densified so that its length contributes to every cell it crosses, then summed per cell. As Table 9 showed, this is a proxy for impervious surface, and it turns out to be the strongest single feature in the model.

major_frac — arterial share

The fraction of total road length in a cell classified as motorway, trunk, primary or secondary. Wide arterial roads collect runoff from a larger area and discharge it to fewer points than a fine residential grid does.

load_per_road — hydraulic load per metre of road

load_per_road = log1p( flow_accumulation ÷ max(road_length, 25) )

This is the only engineered feature, and it came from a question asked explicitly during development: does lower road density mean more standing water? The direct answer is no — Table 9 shows the opposite. But the question pointed at a real physical quantity: load per unit of drainage capacity.

Road length is the only available proxy for drainage capacity, because gullies and catch basins are installed along streets. Flow accumulation is the load. Their ratio is the quantity with physical meaning — how much water arrives per metre of drain available — which density alone does not express. It ranks fifth of nine by importance, validating the reasoning.

Section 7Model Architecture

A gradient-boosted ensemble of shallow decision trees. This section explains what that means before stating how it was configured.

7.1What gradient boosting does

A decision tree splits data by asking threshold questions — is sink depth above 0.4 m? — and following branches to a prediction. A single shallow tree is a weak model: it captures a few coarse rules and little else.

Boosting builds many such trees in sequence, where each new tree is fitted to the errors remaining after all previous trees have had their say. The first tree captures the dominant pattern; the second corrects where the first was wrong; the third corrects what remains, and so on. The final prediction sums the ensemble. Each tree contributes only a small fraction — governed by the learning rate — so the model approaches the solution gradually rather than overshooting.

7.2Why this algorithm and not another

  • Not logistic regression — the relationships are non-linear. Depression depth matters a great deal between 0 and 0.5 m and then saturates; a linear model cannot express that without manual transformation.
  • Not a neural network — 62,494 rows and nine features do not justify one. There is no representation to learn; the features are already the representation.
  • Gradient-boosted trees handle non-linearity and interaction natively, need no feature scaling, and expose a gain attribution per feature that a municipal engineer can be shown as a reason. Auditability was a requirement, not a bonus.

Section 8Validation Design

Two decisions determine whether a reported number means anything. Neither is about the algorithm.

8.1Choosing the negatives

As established in Section 3.6, complaint data is presence-only. There is no record stating "this street did not flood". Negatives must be constructed, and how they are constructed defines the question the classifier ends up answering.

If negatives are drawn from anywhere on the map, they will disproportionately land on parks, water bodies, rail yards and vacant lots — places that carry neither roads nor complaints. Those locations separate trivially from flooded streets, and Figure 7 shows exactly how: as tall isolated spikes where the terrain rasters return constant values. A model trained this way scores well while having learned the question is there a road here?

8.2Spatial blocking

Terrain features are spatially autocorrelated: adjacent cells share slope, catchment and imperviousness. Random k-fold cross-validation therefore places near-identical neighbours in both training and test sets, and the resulting score measures interpolation rather than prediction [11][12].

The protocol used here is GroupKFold with five folds, where the group is a contiguous spatial block of 0.05° ≈ 5.5 km. Whole blocks are held out, so no test cell has a training neighbour.

8.3Leave-one-city-out

Spatial blocking answers whether the model generalises across neighbourhoods. It cannot answer whether it generalises across cities, because sewer design, climate and reporting culture are constant within one city and different in Al Madinah.

The decisive test is therefore leave-one-city-out: train on Chicago, test on New York; then train on New York, test on Chicago. Nothing about the test city is seen during training — not its terrain, not its labels, not its prevalence. This is the closest available analogue to deploying on Al Madinah, and it is the number that decides whether the project rests on anything. Results are in Section 10.2.

Section 9Evaluation Metrics

Every result in this chapter is expressed as a multiple of a baseline. This section builds up to what that multiple means, starting from nothing.

9.1The confusion matrix

A binary classifier's output on a test set can be summarised in four counts:

Model says FLOODModel says NO FLOOD
Actually floodedTrue Positive (TP) — correctly flaggedFalse Negative (FN) — missed it
Not floodedFalse Positive (FP) — false alarmTrue Negative (TN) — correctly ignored

Table 12. The four outcomes.

For a municipality, FN and FP cost different things. A false negative is a street that floods without being on the inspection list. A false positive is a crew sent to a street that turns out fine — wasted time, not damage. The two are not symmetric, which is one reason a single accuracy figure is inadequate.

9.2Precision and recall

Precision = TP / (TP + FP) → of the streets we flagged, how many really flood? Recall = TP / (TP + FN) → of the streets that flood, how many did we flag?

In municipal terms: precision is how much of a crew's day is not wasted; recall is how much of the real problem the list covers. They trade off. Flag every street and recall is perfect while precision equals the base rate. Flag only the single most suspicious street and precision may be perfect while recall is negligible.

9.3Why accuracy fails here

Accuracy is (TP + TN) / total. With 27.4% of New York cells positive, consider the laziest possible classifier — one that ignores every feature and always answers "no flood":

TP = 0, FP = 0, FN = 27.4%, TN = 72.6% Accuracy = 72.6%
A useless model scores 72.6%

It finds nothing, flags nothing, and helps nobody — and it beats many honestly constructed models on accuracy. Any paper or product reporting accuracy on an imbalanced problem is reporting the class balance, not the model.

9.4The precision–recall curve

A classifier outputs a score, not a decision. Turning score into decision requires a threshold, and every threshold gives a different (precision, recall) pair. Sweeping the threshold from strict to permissive traces the precision–recall curve, and the area under it — PR-AUC — summarises performance across all operating points in one number.

9.5The baseline is the prevalence

This is the key to interpreting every number in Section 10. A classifier that assigns random scores achieves, at any recall, a precision equal to the fraction of positives in the data. So its PR-AUC equals the prevalence: 0.274 in New York, 0.358 in Chicago.

Because the baseline differs between cities, raw PR-AUC values are not comparable across them. Chicago's 0.520 is not "better" than New York's 0.396 — Chicago simply floods more often. Dividing each by its own baseline produces a comparable quantity:

lift = PR-AUC ÷ prevalence

A lift of 1.0× means no skill whatsoever. A lift of 1.44× means the model concentrates flooding streets into its top-ranked output 1.44 times more densely than random selection would.

9.6ROC-AUC, and why it is secondary

ROC-AUC plots true positive rate against false positive rate. Its weakness on imbalanced data is that the false positive rate has a large denominator — all the true negatives — so a substantial number of false alarms barely moves it. The metric therefore looks flattering when the negative class dominates [19][20].

ROC-AUC is reported here only for comparability with a literature that overwhelmingly uses it. It is not the metric any conclusion rests on.

9.7What a lift of 1.24× actually buys

Translating the metric into operations

A lift of 1.24–1.30× means that inspecting the top-ranked 10% of street segments finds considerably more genuinely problematic streets than inspecting a random 10% would. It does not mean the top 10% are all problems. Some flooding streets will be missed, and some highly ranked streets will turn out fine.

For an authority with 5,280 km of network and finite crews, moving from random or complaint-driven selection to a ranking with measured lift is a real operational gain. Presenting it as certainty would not be.

10.4The counter-intuitive finding, restated

Table 9 showed that dense road networks flood roughly three times as often as sparse ones, contrary to the team's prior. Table 15 shows why the model relies on it so heavily: road_density is the single most informative column available. Together these say something specific about urban pluvial flooding — that in a city, what has been built matters at least as much as the shape of the ground. This is consistent with the long-standing finding that impervious cover is the dominant control on urban runoff [21], and with the Manhattan anomaly of Section 5.1, where drainage engineering overrode topography entirely.

10.5Comparison with published work

No claim of superiority is made

There is no agreed benchmark for this framing — same descriptor, same cell size, same negative-sampling rule, same validation geometry. Much of the published literature reports ROC-AUC under random splits, which is not comparable with PR-AUC under spatial blocking. A direct numerical comparison would be misleading, so none is offered.

The closest published work using the same New York complaints with TWI, HAND and depression depth is that of Kelleher and McPhillips [6] on citizen-reported urban flooding. The precise claim made here is narrow and, we believe, defensible: the feature set available for Al Madinah carries real signal, under the harshest validation protocol we know how to apply.

11.5The score is a rank, not a probability

The most likely misreading, and the interface says so too

A score of 1.00 means this segment is first in the susceptibility ordering. It does not mean a 100% chance of flooding. The platform prints this on the segments page itself: "the number is a percentile rank, not a flood probability."

It carries a second warning beside it: "what the model does not know: it does not know where drainage networks have actually been built." A segment ranked critical may already have been remediated — which is exactly what the field campaign in Section 14 is designed to correct.

The reporting-bias limitation deserves particular emphasis because it is recursive. The argument for this system is that complaint-driven maintenance produces an inverted picture in which vocal neighbourhoods appear worst. But the training labels are complaints, so some of that same bias is inside the model. The mitigations are partial: the road-bearing negative rule removes the crudest confound, and the cross-city test would penalise a model that had learned a reporting culture specific to one city. Neither eliminates the problem, and the local field campaign is the only real remedy.

Section 14Future Work

14.1Local labels — the decisive step

Al Madinah weights require Al Madinah labels. Two sources are planned:

  1. Self-labelling from the Sentinel-1 radar archive back to 2014. Radar penetrates cloud, so every historical rain event can in principle yield a flooded / not-flooded label for each segment — with no fieldwork at all. This is the more scalable of the two.
  2. A field verification campaign with the municipality on a sample of top-ranked segments after a rain event, measuring the agreement between ranking and reality.

With either, the model can be retrained locally and Al Madinah becomes a contributor rather than a consumer.

14.2Model improvements, in order of expected return

  1. Add dist_to_drain, validated first on New York where catch-basin locations are published.
  2. Re-run on FABDEM or LiDAR to quantify how much of the residual error is terrain coarseness rather than feature weakness.
  3. Add an imperviousness layer to separate the paved-surface effect from the road-geometry effect currently conflated in road_density.

14.3Independent impact measurement

The Kingdom has laid over eight million linear metres of drainage with no independent instrument measuring whether individual schemes worked. Because the satellite archive is retrospective, a before/after comparison is possible for projects already commissioned — a capability that does not currently exist anywhere in the sector, and which requires no new data collection.

Section 15Conclusion

Al Madinah has no record of where its streets flood. That absence is normally treated as a reason not to attempt prediction, or — worse — as something to work around quietly while reporting a confident accuracy figure. This work took the constraint as the research question instead: can a feature set that is computable for an unlabelled city be validated on cities that are labelled, and how much predictive power survives the move?

The answer is that the signal is real and it does transfer, at a measured cost. Nine features derived from one free elevation file and one open street map achieve 1.44× and 1.45× over baseline within cities under spatially blocked validation, and 1.24× and 1.30× when moved to a city never seen in training. Along the way the data contradicted two of the team's expectations: urban form outweighed terrain, and dense street networks flooded three times as often as sparse ones.

What this produces for a municipality is not certainty. It is a ranked list of 30,337 street segments, each with a written reason and a suggested action, covering streets that nobody has ever complained about — available before the season rather than after the storm. For an authority responsible for 5,280 km of network with finite crews, replacing complaint-driven triage with a ranking of measured lift is a real gain.

The honest summary is the one the interface itself prints: the number is a rank, not a probability; the model does not know where drainage has already been built; and no segment in Al Madinah has yet been confirmed on the ground. Those three sentences are not caveats appended to a claim — they are part of the claim, and the field campaign that will begin to retire them is the next piece of work.

Priority-floodAn efficient algorithm for filling depressions in an elevation grid; the fill amount gives depression depth.
Presence-only dataRecords of where something occurred, with no records of where it did not.
PrevalenceThe fraction of cases that are positive — here, the share of cells with a flood report.
PR-AUCArea under the precision–recall curve. The appropriate summary metric for rare events.
LiftPR-AUC divided by prevalence. 1.0× means no skill.
Spatial blockingCross-validation that holds out whole contiguous regions so neighbours cannot leak between train and test.
GainIn gradient boosting, a feature's total contribution to reducing error — used here as importance.
Pluvial floodingRain standing where it fell, as distinct from a river overflowing or a wadi flash flood.
